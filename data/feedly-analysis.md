# What Your Feeds Say About You

*A meta-analysis of 756 Feedly subscriptions across 13 categories.*

---

## The Numbers Don't Lie

Photography: **342 feeds** — 45% of your entire Feedly library. That's not a hobby. That's a worldview. You follow nearly as many individual photographers as some people follow people on Twitter. But look closer: almost none of them are gear blogs, technique tutorials, or camera news. They're personal photo blogs — individual humans with individual eyes, most of them built during the golden age of photo Tumblrs and Blogspot journals (2008–2015). This is someone who curates perspectives, not publications.

Everything else falls into place around that: Design (98), Bikes (67), People (66), News (53), Clothes (39), House (28), Watches (13), Music (12). The ratios are meaningful. You follow more cycling blogs than you follow clothing blogs, which would surprise people who know you primarily as a menswear guy. You follow more "People" (individual personal blogs) than News. You have exactly one Snowboarding feed.

---

## The Pattern That Runs Through All of It

Across every category — bikes, watches, menswear, photography, home, design — the feeds share a single underlying interest: **things made with intention, built to last, worth understanding**.

Not trend coverage. Not fast fashion. Not the algorithm's picks. The cycling feeds you follow are The Radavist, Rene Herse Cycles, Cycle Exif, Escape Collective — publications that treat bikes as objects with histories, not as products to be reviewed and replaced. The watch feeds are Hodinkee, A Collected Man, SJX, worn&wound — editorial outlets that approach horology the way a good biographer approaches a life: with patience and genuine curiosity about craft. The menswear feeds are Die, Workwear!, Permanent Style, Denimhunters, A Continuous Lean — people who write about clothes as social history and material culture, not shopping guides.

The question these feeds implicitly ask, across every category, is: *who made this, and why does it matter?*

That's the same question behind Rene Herse handbuilt touring bikes and Viberg boots and the Maaps incense holders you have from Johan at 3sixteen. The aesthetic isn't "expensive" — it's *considered*.

---

## You Curate People, Not Institutions

A striking structural feature: you subscribe to individuals far more than to outlets. The Photography section is almost entirely personal blogs and Tumblrs of individual photographers. The People section is 66 personal blogs of people you found interesting enough to follow. Your News section has column-specific NYT feeds (Ask Polly, Like a Boss, Choire Sicha) rather than the newspaper itself.

This is a reader who tracks humans, not mastheads. When someone's writing gets interesting, you subscribe to that person. When they leave, the feed stays — a kind of digital memorial to a relationship you had with someone's work at a particular moment.

---

## This Feedly Library Is Partly a Time Capsule

A significant portion of these 756 feeds — especially in Photography and People — were built during the era when personal blogs were social media. Before Instagram killed the photo blog. Before Tumblr's 2018 collapse. Before newsletters ate the indie publishing world.

The Selby, 40 Days of Dating, countless Tumblr-hosted photographers — these were the internet's creative commons in 2010-2014, when following someone's blog was how you stayed close to their work. Many of these feeds are quiet now. Some are dead. But you haven't unsubscribed, which suggests something: either you haven't done a cleanup in years (plausible), or you're holding onto a record of things that mattered to you at a specific time (also plausible, and more interesting).

---

## The Bikes Reveal Something Specific

67 cycling feeds, and almost none of them are about racing. No Tour de France coverage, no power-to-weight ratio discourse. The feeds are: framebuilders (Caletti, Speedvagen, Allied Cycle Works), bikepacking and adventure (Bikepacking.com, Joe Cruz, To Be Determined), crafted motorcycle aesthetics (Iron & Air, Deus Customs, Classified Moto, Iron & Resin), and thoughtful cycling journalism (The Radavist, Escape Collective, Rene Herse).

The interest is in bikes as *objects* — how they're made, what makes them beautiful, the culture and community built around them. The Radavist is the platonic ideal here: cycling photojournalism with a West Coast outdoors sensibility, deeply invested in the aesthetics of the built bike rather than the performance metrics of the athletic one. It's the cycling equivalent of following Die, Workwear! instead of GQ.

Same worldview, different material.

## Japan as Reference Culture

Scattered across categories: STRATO BLOG (Japanese menswear), ShootTokyo, 19 Days in Japan, Iron & Resin (Sonoma brand with deep Japanese selvedge denim DNA), 3sixteen (NYC brand built around Japanese fabrics and construction), Rene Herse (now based in Japan, producing handbuilt randonneuring wheels). Japan shows up as the implicit benchmark for craft — the place where attention to process and material quality is taken most seriously, and where the old ways of making things are still respected enough to be worth preserving.

## The Examined Creative Life

Frank Chimero, Austin Kleon, Are.na Editorial, The Great Discontent, Ways We Work, How to Work with Creative People — a cluster of Design feeds that aren't really about design. They're about *how creative people think and work*. What it means to make something. How you build a practice. What "showing your work" actually requires.

This sits alongside the PKM/Second Brain interests in a way that's worth naming: you're not just interested in productivity systems. You're interested in the examined creative life — the meta-question of how someone with aesthetic sensibilities and analytical capabilities actually organizes themselves to make things that matter. That's what connects Tiago Forte to Frank Chimero to Austin Kleon. It's the same question from different angles.

---

## The Thesis

If someone read your 756 feeds without knowing who you were, here's what they'd conclude:

This is a person who takes the long view on things. Who finds beauty in craft and process. Who wants to understand *how* things are made and *why* they were made that way. Who curates human voices rather than algorithmic feeds. Who has been building this library since before Instagram existed, and still hasn't cleaned it up — which is either a flaw or a feature depending on how you look at it.

The working theory: it's a feature. The accumulation is the point. These 756 feeds aren't a reading list; they're a record of attention over time. A body of work, in subscriptions.

Brick by brick.

## Post-Audit Update — June 2026

*After cross-referencing the full list against Feedly's own unreachability data (389 flagged feeds), the library has been reorganized: 374 active, 377 archived. Some observations that sharpen the original analysis.*

**The time capsule thesis is now a fact, not a theory.** The original analysis speculated that a "significant portion" of feeds were dormant relics from the blogging era. The audit confirms it: 198 of 340 Photography feeds — 58% — are unreachable. The personal photo blog as a living format is effectively dead, and your library is a detailed archaeological record of the people who were making things in that window.

**Photography's dominance is real but revised.** The 45% figure was based on total subscriptions. Active Photography feeds are 142 of 374 (38%) — still the single largest category by a wide margin, but the portrait changes slightly. What's left after removing the dead feeds are the feeds that *survived* the Instagram era: longer-form documentary photographers, editorial publications, a handful of practitioners who found ways to keep publishing. The survivors are, almost without exception, the ones doing work that doesn't fit in a square.

**What survived tells you more than what was there.** The active list reads like a curation of things that couldn't be replaced by Instagram: Radavist long-reads, Escape Collective journalism, Hodinkee essays, Denimhunters deep dives, Permanent Style reviews, A Continuous Lean dispatches. These are all formats where the writing is inseparable from the value. The feeds that died were largely image-only — photographers who moved their work to Instagram because that's where the audience went. You kept the ones where the words mattered.

**Music is the category that didn't make it.** 5 active feeds remain in Music — down from 12. The surviving ones are functional rather than editorial: releases, news, nothing that resembles the early-blog-era music writing you were following at Atlantic and Mindshare. That chapter of professional and personal identity has archived itself. The feeds know before you do.
